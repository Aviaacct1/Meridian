What Meridian does, how to run an assessment, and how to read the numbers.

What it does

Meridian forecasts a new air route. You give it a city pair, an airline, an aircraft and a frequency; it returns the annual traffic each way, the load the aircraft would carry, and the route economics. The demand comes from measured Sabre origin-and-destination traffic and live OAG schedules, not a rule of thumb, so the answer is grounded in what the market actually flies today.

A forecast has three parts: the local market Meridian measures in the origin catchment, the share the new service captures from competing airports and airlines, and the connecting feed the chosen airline adds behind and beyond each end. The three add up to the total each-way forecast.

Why the same route can give two different numbers

Because two different questions are being asked, and both answers are right.

Leave the airline blank. You are asking what the market is worth on its own. Meridian measures how many people already travel between the two places, works out how many of them would use a nonstop, and stops there. Nobody is connecting onto anything, because there is no airline yet. This is the number an airport uses to decide whether a route is worth chasing at all.

Put an airline in, or press Optimise. Now you are asking what that particular airline would carry. On top of the local market they add their own passengers: people flying in from somewhere else to catch the flight, and people carrying on somewhere else at the far end. That is the connecting feed, and it belongs to the airline, not to the route.

So a route can be worth 8,000 passengers to one carrier and 21,000 to another, on the same day, with the same measured market underneath. That is not the model changing its mind. An airline with a big bank of flights at the far end simply has more people to put on the aeroplane, and the whole point of a quality of service index forecast is to measure exactly that difference.

If you are pitching four airlines, expect four forecasts. Send each carrier the one built on their own network. A forecast built on somebody else's connections is not a forecast of their route.

New markets, where nobody flies today

Some routes have almost no measured traffic, because there is no service and the journey is too awkward to make. The demand is real, it just has nowhere to show up in the data.

When that happens Meridian says so on the page, and it stops measuring and starts comparing. It looks at routes that launched into the same kind of gap, sees what they actually carried in their first year, and builds the forecast from that. This is why a new-market forecast can be larger than the market Meridian measured: the measured market is what people manage to do today, and the forecast is what they would do once the flight exists.

A new-market forecast is a comparison, not a measurement, and it carries more uncertainty. The Track Record page shows how the engine has performed on launches of this kind.

Run an assessment

  1. Origin and destination. Type a city or an airport code at each end. A city resolves to its main commercial airport; type the code (GOA, JFK) to pin a specific one. Meridian recognises names as you type.
  2. Airline. Enter the IATA code (BA, LH, W6). The airline matters: it sets the connecting feed, so the same route forecasts higher on a carrier with a strong bank behind the destination. Leave it blank and let Optimise choose.
  3. Carrier type and aircraft. Full service, low cost or ultra low cost sets the cost and fare assumptions. The aircraft list narrows to the airline's own fleet once an airline is set.
  4. Frequency and schedule. Flights each way per week, and whether the service runs year-round or as a summer or winter season. The schedule choice rescales the annual economics to the operating window.
  5. Output. Forecast gives demand and catchment; plus Economics adds the route profit and loss; Full report produces the downloadable deck.
  6. Run, or Optimise. Run assessment forecasts exactly what you entered. Optimise leaves the airline, aircraft or frequency to Meridian and returns the combination with the best route economics.

If the route already operates, Meridian flags it before running and forecasts a new entrant on top of the existing service. The Recent chips under the form reload your last few assessments.

Catchment, Expert and Optimise

Catchment (in the menu) maps a single airport: the resident population within drive time, banded by 30-minute rings, with the total catchment and the share Meridian assumes it captures. Where we hold survey or mobility data, that measured capture replaces the model.

Expert mode exposes every engine variable: the measured market, coverage, capture share, stimulation, catchment radius, and the full connecting-feed and connection settings. Each box shows its calibrated default and range; leave it blank to use the calibrated value, or set it to override. This is for analysts who want to test a specific assumption.

Optimise shortlists the airlines that could plausibly fly the sector, works out each one's demand including its own feed, then picks the airline, aircraft and frequency with the best economics. It only proposes aircraft that are in a carrier's real fleet and can make the distance.

Where the numbers come from, and what to watch

Demand is measured from Sabre origin-and-destination traffic and sized to the origin catchment; schedules and served airports come from live OAG. Capture is calibrated against launched routes, so it reflects how markets have actually behaved rather than a fixed assumption. The economics use validated aircraft operating costs, maintenance reserves and ownership cost by airline type and age.

Treat the output as a considered estimate, not a guarantee. It is strongest where the market has real Sabre history and weakest on thin or brand-new markets. A forecast on a secondary airport depends heavily on its capture share, which is why measured catchment data, where we have it, matters more than the model. When in doubt, open Expert mode and test how far the answer moves when you flex the assumption you are least sure of.

Questions or something that looks wrong: note the city pair, airline and the figure, and send it to the Avia team. This is a prototype and the calibration keeps improving as more routes are added.

THE AXIS IS THE NUMBER OF AIRPORTS THE LICENCE COVERS, which John reached
himself. Five shapes were proposed across 20 September and four withdrawn on
John's rulings: tiers carrying the long-term forecast (it is a separate software
product); tiers carrying researched-pack blocks (a pack limit stops the client
refining and adds development complexity, and the pack is the part of Meridian
easiest for anyone to copy); tiers by seat count (no airport needs more than five
and most run two or three, so seats have no range, and the brand skin will not
carry a price either); and a flat per-airport rate (RDC at circa 12,000 buys
global airport charges and OAG at circa 20,000 buys global access, so a flat rate
becomes expensive against that shelf very quickly).

THE LICENCE: three bands by airports covered, 15,000 for one airport, 22,500 for
two to nine, 30,000 for ten or more. Two of the three are John's own prices from
item 37, so the downstream change is the smallest of any shape considered, and
the first two launch rows are arithmetic W5 has already built and verified. Every
band carries the whole product with unlimited runs, scenarios, exports and
researched packs under a fair-use clause, no seat count and no seat charge, named
users on the operator's corporate email domain, and no consultant ever holding a
seat. Analysis of any city pair is unrestricted on every licence; only production,
being the researched pack, the client's brand, route leads, the opportunity scan
and Watch, is scoped to covered airports, with the check on the two output
endpoints and a condition of the first multi-airport contract rather than of
Routes. Groups are quoted and the bands are never published beside the
single-airport price, which is John's own decision of 3 August.

Cross-check recorded: every band lands at roughly 5,000 to 7,500 per person doing
air service development, so seats and estate are two independent readings arriving
at the same prices.

Final ruling of the day from John: the single-airport list starts at 15,000 for
Routes, quoted and unpublished, and a smaller airport band can be introduced
later if the evidence supports it. Nothing in the 2026 material carries a
passenger threshold, so item 37's ruling that size is not a pricing axis stands.

Knock is recorded as the worked example of the small airport and as a second
sourced anchor for the study price: Avia is employed every two years or so for
one forecast at circa 5,000 or about six for circa 20,000, which with Tampa's
40,000 dollars for ten puts an Avia consultancy route report at circa 3,300 to
5,000. A Meridian study at 3,500 sits at the bottom of that range and undercuts
the single-forecast price by 30 per cent. Knock is not a licence buyer at any
price in this file, because its spend is circa 10,000 a year and episodic; it is
a study buyer, and that is the correct outcome. The crossover is corrected: four
studies is 14,000 and five is 17,500, so past four routes a year the
single-airport licence is cheaper, not past six.

A sampling caution is recorded with a November action: airports of Knock's shape
do not attend World Routes because they cannot afford it, so feedback collected
at Frankfurt will over-represent airports that can, and the small-airport band
cannot be settled from the show alone. Five telephone calls to existing small
clients from Avia's own book is the fix.

Knock anchors corrected and extended, all tentative, all John's of 20 September:
circa 5,000 for one forecast, circa 20,000 for about six, and 6,000 for five
HIGH-LEVEL forecasts without presentations. Those are different products and the
gap between them is the finding: a forecast alone is circa 1,200 and a finished
job circa 3,300 to 5,000, so roughly 70 per cent of what a small airport pays
Avia is the presentation, which matches John's statement that most of Avia's time
on a Knock job goes there. That qualifies the earlier reading of the researched
pack: it is reproducible by a group with its own analysts and it is also the
majority of what a small client pays for, because a small airport will never build
a research pipeline.

Whether Knock is a licence buyer is reopened as a question with a cheap test
attached. THE KNOCK TEST, recommended for September rather than November: run
Meridian on a route Knock cares about, send the pack, and ask whether it would
have replaced the hand work and what they would pay a year. If the pack is not
good enough for a client who already buys the hand-made version, that is the most
important product finding in the programme and it should not wait. If the answer
is 10,000 a year, the mechanism is a multi-year prepay or quarterly payment,
never a passenger band.

A bare forecast at circa 1,200, workbook only, is recorded as a possible third
product and is NOT proposed. The niche of small airports that do not buy today is
recorded with what it would be worth: fifty of them at 10,000 is 500,000 a year,
roughly doubling the independent licence revenue, a 2027 market entirely
dependent on the pack being good enough.

Section 2a added, and it is the most consequential entry of the session for W3,
W4 and W6. John, 20 September: the researched pack is NOT as good as a handmade
Avia study, which carried pages of detailed research from an expert analyst in
the client's preferred format, adjusted on their feedback; but a client may
accept a lower but good output that lets them run twenty routes a year rather
than a handful. The claim is therefore not "as good as a consultant, cheaper" but
"not as good, and many times as many". The forecast is the strong claim with the
accuracy record behind it and the pack is the volume claim, and the two are never
mixed in one sentence. In Knock's own numbers: about six by hand for circa 20,000,
or unlimited for 15,000.

Two consequences recorded. The positioning protects Avia's consultancy book
rather than competing with it, because an airport running twenty routes finds two
or three worth doing properly and those come back to Avia by hand, so Meridian
creates consultancy demand. And unlimited packs are now settled twice over, on
cost and on positioning, since a cap would destroy the only proposition there is.

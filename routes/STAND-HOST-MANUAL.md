## 2. The three classes of number

This section is the one piece of method you carry cold. It answers most method questions
honestly without going near a coefficient, and it gives you a clean stopping point when a
question goes past it.

Source: Nick Oldrini's methodology note of 23 August 2026, section 2 (internal, private and
confidential; it carries Avia's own calibrated constants and never leaves the firm).

### 2.1 The three classes

"Every number in a Meridian forecast belongs to one of three classes, and we show you which."

- **Measured.** Read from data, not assumed. The addressable market, the schedules in the
  choice set, the sector distance.
- **Calibrated.** Fitted to what launched routes actually did. The capture-share weighting on
  frequency and connection quality, the coverage correction, the stimulation uplift, the
  connecting-feed scoring.
- **Physics.** A hard constraint, not an opinion. The aircraft and frequency cap, the
  achievable load factor, the airfield check.

Why it matters, in your words: "A measured number and a calibrated number carry different
kinds of uncertainty, and a physics cap is not negotiable. If you want to test a forecast, the
first question worth asking is which class a number belongs to."

### 2.2 What to say about the data, without listing suppliers

"Four traffic sources and one population source, each with one job. True origin and
destination passenger volumes for the measured market. Airline schedules for frequency, seats
and connection banks, never for passengers. Airport traffic actuals as a cross-check outside
the United States. United States government data, mandatory for every US airport, both as the
passenger source and as the validation source, because it is the record a US client can check
independently. Population and drive times for the catchment."

Name a supplier only if the visitor names it first. Our licence position allows us to use the
tool at Routes and to share its outputs; it does not make us the right people to discuss
another company's terms on a stand.

### 2.3 The accuracy wording

Say this and only this, word for word:

> Calibrated leads are within 20% of the outcome 89% of the time and within 10% 82% of the
> time, on 2,915 real launches; blind results are reported as portfolios only, never as a
> single route.

`[SLOT: the one sentence that says what the 89% describes. John and Nick, umbrella item 25,
due 26 September. Until it lands, do not explain the figures beyond the wording above. If a
visitor presses on what the figures describe, section 7.2 gives you the answer that holds
today.]`

Three rules around it. Never quote a single-route blind figure, because none exists that is
honest. Never round, restate or improve the wording. Never attach it to the number on the
screen in front of the visitor until the slot above is filled.

### 2.4 Where you stop

When a method question goes past section 2, stop and hand it over. The line:

"John can take you through the method in detail, shall I set that up."

Then take the details on the capture form and mark it as a method follow-up. Stopping here
reads as confidence, because it is. A host who guesses at a coefficient costs us the sale and
the credibility behind it.

## 4. What not to say

Five bans. Each one exists because it has cost someone a sale or a client.

### 4.1 No feature that is not in the frozen build

The build freezes on 10 October and what you demonstrate on 21 October is that build. Three
things visitors ask for that Meridian does not do yet, and the honest answer to each:

- **Day of week.** Frequency comes out as a weekly count. It is not yet allocated across
  specific days. "Not yet. It gives you the weekly frequency the demand supports, not which
  days to fly. That work is next."
- **Fare in the capture score.** "Fares sit in the economics, not yet inside the capture
  score. On the roadmap."
- **The newest aircraft types' economics.** "The operating economics for the newest types are
  still being built. Everything in the current fleet is there."

Say "not yet" and "on the roadmap". Never say "it can do that" about any of the three, and
never say "it will be ready by" any date. A client who is told is a beta client; a client who
finds out is a lost one.

Two further caveats from the methodology note, for a visitor who works in this field: the
split between local and connecting passengers is under active review and can move between two
otherwise identical runs, and a fare figure and a demand figure occasionally sit on a
different each-way or two-way basis unless the report states which. Meridian's reports state
the basis. Offer this rather than hide it; the people who care are the people who buy.

### 4.2 No price beyond the one sentence

Section 7.5 carries the whole of what you say about money. No discount figure, no number of
places, no expiry date, no hint at any of the three, until John rules on them.

### 4.3 No single-route blind accuracy figure

None exists that is honest. The wording in section 2.3 is the complete answer.

### 4.4 No comparison with any competitor

We do not name a competitor, we do not compare, and we do not evaluate one. Nothing about any
competitor goes in the deck, the pack, the website or a post.

If a visitor names one, you have one sentence and then you listen:

`[SLOT: the one honest sentence, drafted below by W4 and awaiting John's approval of the
wording.]`

> Drafted for approval: "We do this differently in three ways: we publish how close we have
> been on real launches, we work out what time of day the route should fly, and every visitor
> leaves with a researched pack for their own route. Twenty-five years of building these
> forecasts by hand is behind all three."

Then stop and listen. "What do you use it for? What does it do well? What do you wish it did?"
Capture every word of the answer in the notes field. That is feedback arriving for free,
without us having asked for it, and it is worth more than the argument would have been. Do not
counter anything the visitor says about their own tool.

If the visitor works for a competitor, be courteous, run nothing, and take no details. "Good
to meet you. I am afraid I am not going to demonstrate it for you, but do come and say hello
to John."

### 4.5 No number defended on the stand

When the tool disagrees with the visitor, section 7.6 is the whole answer, and it is an
opportunity rather than a problem. You never defend a figure and you never adjust one to
please someone.

## 7. Objections and answers

One page. Learn the shape of each answer, not the words, except where the words are ruled.

### 7.1 "We have our own model"

"Good, then you will read this quickly. Most teams we work with have a workbook someone built
and nobody else can run. Two questions worth asking of it: how long does it take you to test a
different aircraft or a different departure time, and what do you say when the airline asks
how accurate it has been? Let me show you both on your own route."

Then run it. A team with its own model is a qualified buyer, not a lost one.

### 7.2 "How accurate is it really"

The wording in section 2.3, said once, unhurried. Then:

"Three things behind it. It is tested out of sample, so the model is scored only on launches
from years it never saw. US launches are checked against the US government's own survey and
everything else against global booking data, and those two references do not always agree with
each other on the same route, which is one reason we do not quote a single-route figure as a
promise. A portfolio of candidate routes is called more accurately than any one route, so if
you are ranking a network case, that is the easier and better-supported question."

If the visitor asks whether the figures in section 2.3 describe the number on this screen: `[SLOT: the settled
sentence, umbrella item 25, due 26 September.]` Until it lands: "Those figures belong to the
calibration record behind the tool. What is on this screen is the step-by-step build, and it
carries its own calibrated range, which you can see here. John can take you through exactly
how the two relate, shall I set that up." Do not go further than that.

### 7.3 "Where does the data come from"

Section 2.2, in your own words. Four traffic sources and one population source, each with one
job, and the US government data as the independently checkable record for every US airport.
Name a supplier only if the visitor names it first.

### 7.4 "Can it do day of week, or fares"

"Not yet, and I would rather tell you now than after you have bought it. Frequency comes out
as a weekly count, not allocated to specific days. Fares are in the economics, not yet inside
the capture score. Both are on the roadmap, and the known-issues list goes out with the
onboarding, not after it."

Write what they asked for on the capture form. These answers are the product roadmap for
November.

### 7.5 "What does it cost"

Say the published structure only:

> £15,000, £20,000 or £25,000 a year by airport size. Three named seats. 100 generated
> presentations included. Launch places this year on request.

Then stop. If the visitor asks which band they are in: "Size comes from published passenger
numbers and we confirm it in the quotation; we do not print the thresholds." If they ask for a
discount, a number of places or a deadline: "John handles that. Shall I book you a follow-up
with him." No figure, no number of places, no expiry, and no rough one either.

The written offer lives in a one-pager that John sends to qualified visitors within 48 hours of
the show. It never travels on the stand and it is not in the pack, so do not hand it over, read
from it or promise its contents.

If the visitor wants to sign on the stand: take the details, thank them properly, and tell them
the truth. "Onboarding opens on 7 November and you are first in the queue." A signature taken
before we can support it is a support problem, not a sale.

### 7.6 "The forecast is wrong, we know our market"

This one is the product working, and you say so.

"A gap is informative. Which side do you think is right, and why?"

Then listen and write down the reason, in their words, on the capture form. Ask what they know
that the data does not: a charter that does not book through the global systems, a visiting
friends and relatives market, a fare deal, a road or rail link that changed. Offer the
follow-up: "That conversation is exactly the one worth having with John, and the pack will
give you both something to argue over."

Never defend the number. Never adjust it to please someone. A host who argues loses; a host
who asks why gets the market intelligence and the meeting.

### 7.7 "Your airport is not in there" or the run looks wrong

"The data is thin for that market. Let me show you a neighbour, and we will run yours properly
and send it to you."

Then run the neighbour, capture the request, and mark it for a person to check before the pack
goes. Never show a visitor a number the tool has flagged and call it a forecast.

If an airport or a city name will not resolve at all, do not retype it repeatedly in front of
the visitor. Enter the airport code, or move to the neighbour and capture the request.

### 7.8 "Who else uses it"

"We are launching it here. The forecasting behind it has been in client work for twenty-five
years, and the calibration record behind it is the wording in section 2.3. We are taking a
small number of launch clients this year, and John will tell you where that stands." No names, no logos, no
implication that a client exists where one does not.

### 7.9 "Can I have a trial"

"Yes, trials are free and we take no payment details. Let me capture you now and John will set
it up." Do not promise a start date or a length.

### 7.10 "Those operating costs are not ours"

They are right, and the tool says so itself. "The economics are indicative and built on generic
published assumptions, not on any airline's actual costs. They are there so you can see the
shape of the route's profitability and compare one aircraft against another. If you want it run
on a carrier's own cost base, that is a conversation with John."

Never adjust a cost figure on the stand to match what a visitor tells you. Write what they said
on the form; a route development team quoting a real airline cost base is telling us something

### 8.5 The five feedback questions

After each substantive demonstration you answer these five, not the visitor. Thirty seconds

stand card, `W5-FEEDBACK-CARD-19Sep2026.md` v0.1; the same five questions sit behind the capture

Visitor, company, route run, date and session, then:

1. Which route did they run, and why that one?
2. What impressed them? Their words, not ours.
3. What did they question or push back on?
4. What did they ask for that we do not have?
5. Would they pay for it? Cold, warm, or a launch candidate, and what they said that puts them
   there.
